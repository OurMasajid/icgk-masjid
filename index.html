If you're skeptical

A note to anyone who's wary of Muslims

If you came to this website suspicious of Islam — or of people like us — we're genuinely glad you're here.

We know what's out there. Terrible things have been done by people claiming to act in the name of our faith, and those images are hard to forget. They horrified us too. If those images are most of what you've seen of Muslims, it would be strange if you weren't uneasy.

So we won't call you names, and we won't ask you to take our word for anything. We'll ask for one thing: judge us by what you see for yourself. Read our answers to the hardest questions. Look up the sources. Then, if you're willing, come by. Sit at the back during a prayer. Ask whatever is on your mind. Nobody will pressure you to believe anything — our own scripture says, There is no compulsion in religion (Qur'an 2:256).

You may still disagree with us when you leave. That's all right. Neighbors don't have to agree on everything — but they should know each other.

— Your neighbors at the Islamic Community of Greater Killeen

Where we stand, in plain words

We condemn terrorism. No “buts.”

Killing innocent people is one of the gravest sins in Islam — whoever does it, and whatever cause they claim.

We keep the law of the land.

The Qur'an commands believers to honor their commitments (5:1). Living here as citizens and residents is one of them.

We believe in freedom of religion — for everyone.

The First Amendment protects your church, synagogue, or no faith at all, exactly as it protects our masjid.

We want to be good neighbors.

The Prophet Muhammad taught that a person whose neighbor isn't safe from them is not a true believer (Bukhari).

Hard questions, honest answers

Ask us the things you've heard.

These are questions people really ask us — sometimes politely, sometimes not. Here are short, straight answers. The full page goes deeper and cites every source, so you can check it yourself instead of trusting us.

Read all the questions Ask your own
Does Islam teach Muslims to be violent toward non-Muslims?

No. The Qur'an permits fighting only in self-defense or to stop oppression, and it sets limits even then: Fight those who fight you, but do not transgress (2:190). Toward peaceful people of other faiths, it commands kindness and fairness (60:8).

The verses quoted online to suggest otherwise are almost always cut from their context — a specific 7th-century war against tribes that had broken their treaties. Read the verse right after the famous “sword verse”: it orders Muslims to grant safe passage to any enemy who asks for it (9:6). Full answer

Then why don't Muslims speak out against terrorism?

We do — constantly. It just rarely makes the news. In 2005, the Fiqh Council of North America issued a religious ruling that terrorism is forbidden in Islam. In 2014, more than 120 Muslim scholars published an open letter to the leader of ISIS refuting his group, point by point, from Islamic sources.

And we'll say it here, plainly: terrorism is a crime against God and against people. Those who commit it do not represent Islam, Muslims, or this masjid. Full answer

Do Muslims want to replace American law with Sharia?

No. For Muslims, “Sharia” mostly means the guidance we live by personally: praying, fasting, giving charity, eating halal, keeping our word, being honest in business and faithful in marriage. None of that needs a law passed.

Mainstream Islamic scholarship teaches that Muslims must keep the laws of the country they live in. And we value the Constitution: the First Amendment protects our freedom to worship exactly as it protects yours. Full answer

Can a devout Muslim be a loyal American?

Yes. Muslim Americans serve in every branch of the U.S. armed forces, and fallen Muslim service members rest at Arlington National Cemetery. They also serve as doctors, nurses, police officers, teachers and small-business owners in towns like ours.

In Islam, keeping your word is a religious duty — and that includes an oath of citizenship or enlistment. Full answer

Doesn't Islam oppress women?

Some governments and cultures in Muslim-majority countries do treat women unjustly, and many Muslims — especially Muslim women — are among their loudest critics. Those practices contradict Islam's own teachings.

The Prophet's first wife, Khadijah, was a successful businesswoman who employed him and proposed to him. Islam requires a woman's consent to marry and gives her the right to own property and keep her own earnings. The Prophet said, The best of you are those who are best to their wives (Tirmidhi). Better yet, ask the women in our community yourself. Full answer

Isn't “taqiyya” a license for Muslims to lie to non-Muslims?

No. Taqiyya is a narrow allowance: someone facing torture or death for their faith may hide it to survive (16:106). It has nothing to do with deceiving neighbors, and it doesn't apply to a website.

Islam treats lying as a serious sin. The Prophet said truthfulness leads to goodness and lying leads to wrongdoing (Bukhari, Muslim). That's why we cite our sources — check them yourself. Full answer

Visit

Your first visit, made easy

You don't need to be Muslim, know anything about Islam, or call ahead — though you're welcome to. Here's what to expect.

1

Come at a prayer time

Arrive a few minutes before an iqāmah time. Friday's Jumu'ah at 1:30 PM is our biggest gathering of the week.

2

Dress modestly

Loose, comfortable clothes that cover arms and legs. Women may wish to bring a light scarf to cover their hair in the prayer hall.

3

Shoes off at the door

We pray on the carpet, so shoes come off before you step into the prayer hall. Clean socks are a plus.

4

Watch, ask, or just sit

You won't be asked to take part. Sit toward the back during the prayer, then say hello — we're glad to answer questions.

Who we are

A local masjid with a growing family

The Islamic Community of Greater Killeen (ICGK) is a masjid — the Arabic word for mosque — serving Muslims in Killeen, Harker Heights, Copperas Cove and the Fort Hood area.

We're a place of prayer, learning, and friendship. Whether you're a lifelong resident, new to town, a soldier stationed nearby, or simply curious, our doors are open to you. We gather for the five daily prayers, Friday Jumu'ah, Ramadan and the two Eids, with programs all year for every age.

Our community is growing, and we're working to expand the masjid so we can serve everyone better. We'd love for you to be part of it.
