Hard Questions, Honest Answers

These are the questions people ask about Islam when they're uneasy, suspicious — or angry. We've answered them as plainly as we can, and cited our sources so you can check them yourself instead of taking our word for it.

Before we start

A few promises we've made to ourselves in writing this page:

  • We won't dodge. If a question is hard, we'll say so and answer it anyway.
  • We won't pretend Muslims never do wrong. Some do, as people of every faith do — and we'll call it wrong.
  • We'll show our sources. Qur'an verses link to Quran.com, where you can compare several published translations. The quotations here are plain-English renderings of the meaning. Sayings of the Prophet (hadith) are cited by the classical collection they come from.

Violence & extremism

Does Islam teach Muslims to be violent toward non-Muslims?

No. The Qur'an permits fighting only to defend against aggression or to end oppression — and it sets limits even then:

“Fight in the cause of God those who fight you, but do not transgress. God does not love transgressors.”Qur'an 2:190

Toward people who live in peace with Muslims, the instruction is kindness and fairness:

“God does not forbid you from being kind and just to those who have not fought you over your faith or driven you from your homes. God loves those who are just.”Qur'an 60:8

The Prophet Muhammad forbade the killing of women and children in war (Bukhari, Muslim), and his successor Abu Bakr ordered his armies not to kill the elderly, cut down fruit trees, or destroy homes (Muwaṭṭaʾ Mālik). The Qur'an repeatedly condemns fasād — spreading corruption and destruction on the earth (28:77).

Do some Muslims commit violence? Yes — as some people of every faith and of none do. The real question is whether the religion teaches it. Read the verses in full and in context, and we think you'll find it doesn't.

What about the verses that say “kill them wherever you find them”?

Those words appear in Qur'an 9:5, with similar wording in 2:191. Quoted alone, they sound terrifying — and online, they're almost always quoted alone. Here's what surrounds them:

  • They're about a specific war. Chapter 9 addresses tribes in 7th-century Arabia that had signed peace treaties with the Muslims, broken them, and attacked. The chapter says so itself: “Will you not fight a people who broke their oaths… and attacked you first?” (9:13)
  • Those who kept their treaties were exempt. “Honor your treaty with them until its term ends. God loves those who are mindful of Him.” (9:4)
  • The very next verse offers protection. “If any of them asks you for protection, grant it, so that they may hear the word of God — then escort them to a place where they are safe.” (9:6)
  • 2:191 is bracketed by limits. It follows “do not transgress” (2:190) and is followed by “if they stop, then God is forgiving and merciful” (2:192).

Every scripture with a long history contains passages about war, and any of them can be made to sound like a standing order by cutting away the verses around it. Mainstream Muslim scholarship reads these verses as rules for that particular conflict — which is also how the overwhelming majority of the world's Muslims, who live peacefully alongside their neighbors, understand them.

Why don't Muslims condemn terrorism?

They do — loudly and often. It just rarely makes headlines. A few examples you can look up:

  • 2005: The Fiqh Council of North America, a body of Islamic legal scholars in the U.S. and Canada, issued a fatwa (religious ruling) declaring that there is no justification in Islam for terrorism or extremism. Major American Muslim organizations endorsed it.
  • 2004–2005: The Amman Message, endorsed by hundreds of leading scholars from across the Muslim world, rejected extremism and the practice of declaring other Muslims unbelievers — the excuse extremist groups use to justify killing them.
  • 2014: More than 120 Muslim scholars from around the world signed an open letter to the leader of ISIS, refuting his group's ideology point by point from the Qur'an and Islamic law.

It's also worth knowing who the victims usually are. A 2011 U.S. National Counterterrorism Center report found that, where the victims' religion could be determined, Muslims made up between 82 and 97 percent of terrorism-related deaths over the previous five years.

And here is our own statement: terrorism is a grave crime against God and against human beings. Those who commit it do not represent Islam, Muslims, or this masjid. We condemn it without conditions, whoever the victims are.

What does “jihad” really mean?

Jihad means “striving” or “struggle.” Most of the time, Muslims use it for the everyday effort to do right: resisting our own worst impulses, raising our children well, standing up for justice. The Prophet said the best jihad is “a word of truth spoken to an unjust ruler” (Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi).

It can also refer to armed defense — and we won't pretend otherwise. Like the Christian just-war tradition, Islamic law surrounds it with strict rules: it may only be declared by a legitimate authority, never by individuals or militias; it exists to defend and to end oppression; and civilians — women, children, the elderly, and clergy — may never be targeted.

Terrorist groups break every one of those rules, then borrow the word to make murder sound religious. Muslim scholars have been saying so for decades.

What does “Allahu Akbar” actually mean?

It means “God is greater” — greater than anything else, and greater than us. It's one of the most ordinary phrases in a Muslim's life. It opens the call to prayer and every prayer itself, so Muslims say it dozens of times a day. Many parents whisper the call to prayer, which begins with it, into the ear of a newborn baby. People say it when they see something beautiful or hear good news.

When a criminal shouts it during an attack, it horrifies us. They are taking the words of our worship and attaching them to the opposite of everything those words mean. (Arabic-speaking Christians also call God “Allah” — it's simply the Arabic word for God.)

Law & country

Do Muslims want Sharia law in America?

No. Sharia literally means “the path to water” — the guidance Muslims follow in life. In practice, the great majority of it is about personal life: how to pray, fast, give charity, eat, marry, run an honest business, and treat your parents. Living by Sharia in Killeen looks like praying on time, eating halal, keeping your word, and paying your debts. None of that requires changing a single American law.

Mainstream Islamic scholarship teaches that Muslims living in a country are bound by their agreement with it, and must keep its laws. The Qur'an is direct about this:

“O you who believe, fulfill your commitments.”Qur'an 5:1
“Keep your promises — you will be asked about every promise.”Qur'an 17:34

We are also among the biggest beneficiaries of America's protections for religion. The First Amendment protects our masjid exactly as it protects your church or synagogue, and it keeps the government from imposing any religion on anyone. We have every reason to defend it — for everyone.

Can a devout Muslim be a loyal American?

Yes. Muslims have been part of America since before it was a country — many of the enslaved Africans brought to these shores were Muslim. Thomas Jefferson owned a copy of the Qur'an, and the 1796 Treaty of Tripoli, approved unanimously by the U.S. Senate, stated that the United States had “no character of enmity against the laws, religion, or tranquility, of Mussulmen.”

Today, Muslim Americans serve in every branch of the U.S. armed forces, and fallen Muslim service members are buried at Arlington National Cemetery. Muslim Americans also work as doctors, nurses, police officers, teachers, and small-business owners in towns across the country.

In Islam, keeping your word is an act of faith — and that includes the oath of citizenship and the oath of enlistment. A Muslim who betrays their country isn't being more religious. They're being less.

Isn't “taqiyya” a license for Muslims to lie?

No. This is one of the most repeated claims about Muslims online, and it's simply wrong.

Taqiyya refers to a narrow allowance: a person facing torture or death for their faith may conceal it to survive. Its basis is Qur'an 16:106, revealed about early Muslims who were tortured in Makkah. It has nothing to do with deceiving neighbors, coworkers, or the public, and it is not a strategy for spreading Islam.

On honesty itself, Islam is blunt:

“Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise… Lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire.”The Prophet Muhammad, reported by Bukhari and Muslim

Before he was a prophet, Muhammad was known in Makkah as al-Amīn — “the trustworthy” — and even his opponents left their valuables in his care.

We know you can't verify our sincerity from a webpage. That's why every claim here has a source you can check — and why we'd rather you come meet us.

Neighbors & everyday life

Doesn't Islam oppress women?

We won't pretend this question comes from nowhere. Some governments and cultures in Muslim-majority countries treat women unjustly: forced marriages, girls kept out of school, laws that make women second-class citizens. Many Muslims — Muslim women most of all — are among the strongest critics of those practices, because they contradict what Islam actually teaches:

  • Consent. A woman may not be married without her consent (Bukhari, Muslim).
  • Property and earnings. A woman owns her own property, income, and dowry — what she earns is hers (Qur'an 4:32).
  • Education. The Prophet taught that seeking knowledge is a duty on every Muslim (Ibn Mājah) — and scholars have always understood that to include women.
  • Character. “The best of you are those who are best to their wives” (Tirmidhi).
  • Example. The Prophet's first wife, Khadijah, was a successful merchant who hired him and proposed marriage to him. His wife Aisha became one of the most important scholars of early Islam, teaching men and women alike.

Equal in worth doesn't always mean identical in role, and Muslims discuss — and sometimes disagree about — how these teachings apply today, as people of every faith do. If you want to know what Islam is like for women, the best people to ask are Muslim women. You're welcome to ask the women of our community yourself.

Is the hijab forced on women?

It shouldn't be. The Qur'an says “there is no compulsion in religion” (2:256), and an act of worship done under force loses its meaning. Most Muslim women who wear the hijab describe it as a personal act of devotion and modesty — much like a nun's habit, or the head covering many Orthodox Jewish women wear. The Qur'an asks men to be modest too, in how they look at others and carry themselves (24:30).

Forcing a woman to wear it — or forcing her to take it off — takes away her choice. Many Muslims oppose both. More about the hijab

What do Muslims think of Christians and Jews?

The Qur'an calls Jews and Christians the “People of the Book” — fellow believers in the God of Abraham who received earlier scripture. Muslims believe in Moses and Jesus as prophets of God; we believe in Jesus' virgin birth and his miracles. Mary is the only woman mentioned by name in the Qur'an, and a whole chapter is named after her.

Islam allows Muslims to eat the food of Jews and Christians, and allows Muslim men to marry Jewish and Christian women (5:5) — rules that would make no sense in a religion that taught hatred of them. The Qur'an instructs:

“Do not argue with the People of the Book except in the best way… and say: ‘We believe in what was revealed to us and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is one.’”Qur'an 29:46

We do have real theological differences — most importantly, Muslims do not believe Jesus is divine. We think neighbors can disagree honestly about theology and still respect each other. Hatred of Jews or Christians has no place in Islam or in this masjid, and we stand against antisemitism just as we ask others to stand against hatred of Muslims.

Are you trying to convert me?

We'd be lying if we said we didn't believe Islam is true — we do, just as a sincere Christian believes Christianity is true. And if you're interested, we're always glad to talk about it.

But no, we won't pressure you. Faith that's forced isn't faith: “There is no compulsion in religion” (2:256). If you visit, nobody is going to corner you. Our goal is simpler: that you leave knowing us a little better than when you came.

What if I just don't like Islam?

That's your right. In America you're free to criticize any religion, including ours — and we'd defend that freedom.

All we ask is what we ask of ourselves: that disagreement stays disagreement. Criticize ideas as much as you like; just don't let it turn into hatred or harm toward people. The Qur'an gives Muslims the same instruction:

“Do not let hatred of a people lead you to be unjust. Be just — that is closer to righteousness.”Qur'an 5:8

We try to live by that, including toward people who don't like us. And if you ever want to talk it over — by phone, at the masjid, or in our Islam! Get the Facts class — the door is open.

Is it safe for me to visit?

Yes. You'll be our guest, and in Islam guests are honored — the Prophet said, “Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him honor his guest” (Bukhari, Muslim). Come at a prayer time or call ahead, and someone will be glad to show you around. If you'd feel more comfortable bringing a friend, bring a friend.
